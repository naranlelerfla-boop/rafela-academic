90 Seconds to Midnight: A Reflection on Global Governance and Climate Inaction

Rafela Naranlele · February 2026

The Doomsday Clock, maintained by the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, currently stands at 85 seconds to midnight—the closest it has ever been to symbolic global catastrophe (Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, 2026).

Climate change remains a defining factor behind this assessment. The Paris Agreement established the objective of limiting global warming to well below 2°C and pursuing efforts to restrict it to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels (UNFCCC, 2016). However, the Emissions Gap Report 2025 projects that, under current pledges, global warming could still reach approximately 2.3–2.5°C by the end of the century (UNEP, 2025).

According to the Global Carbon Project, fossil carbon dioxide emissions are projected to reach record levels in 2025 (Global Carbon Project, 2025), underscoring the persistent gap between climate ambition and implementation.

This discrepancy reflects structural limitations within global governance. Climate change is fundamentally a coordination challenge in a fragmented international system shaped by national interests, short political cycles, and fossil fuel dependency.

The consequences are already observable. The year 2025 ranked among the hottest on record, with oceans absorbing unprecedented levels of heat and accelerating sea-level rise (Carbon Brief, 2025). Coastal regions and small island states face increasing vulnerability due to sea-level rise and intensified climate impacts (IPCC, 2022).

Climate action, therefore, is not solely an environmental issue. It is a governance question. Until governance systems evolve to match the scale and speed of climate risk, the Clock will continue to reflect not inevitability, but delay.
