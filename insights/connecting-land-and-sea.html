Connecting Land and Sea: A Reflection on Waste Management in Bali

Rafela Naranlele · February 2026

Bali is often celebrated for its cultural richness and breathtaking coastlines. For many, it represents beauty, ritual, and escape. Yet each year, particularly during the west monsoon season, that image is interrupted by waves of marine debris accumulating along its shores.

Ocean currents do carry debris toward the southern coast of Bali, including tourism areas such as Kuta and Jimbaran. However, currents alone do not create waste. The debris that reaches the shoreline is ultimately linked to land-based waste that has not been effectively contained, reduced, or processed.

As tourism expands and consumption patterns shift, waste generation continues to increase. Infrastructure and management capacity, however, have struggled to keep pace. When land-based systems fail to function efficiently, waste travels downstream—into rivers and eventually into the sea.

The Suwung landfill, serving Denpasar and the regencies of Badung, Gianyar, and Tabanan, illustrates the structural complexity of the issue. In this context, waste management becomes more than a technical matter; it reflects how planning decisions and infrastructural readiness do not always move in synchrony.

Ultimately, Bali’s marine debris problem reminds us that land and sea cannot be treated as separate domains. What appears along the shoreline is the visible endpoint of upstream processes. Long-term sustainability depends not only on clean-up efforts, but on coherent governance, adequate infrastructure, and sustained collective responsibility.
